I have built things that worked. I have watched them fall apart too.

The hardest part wasn't telling people a startup had failed. It was sitting with myself afterward, wondering if I was actually good at any of this.

I started young. At fifteen, I built a social network for kids with a small team. We got attention, even raised a little money. I was also trying to finish school, and we couldn't keep the site running. We shut it down before my tenth-grade exams.

I told myself I would learn and do better next time.

A few years later, I joined a laundry startup. We built the product and saw people use it. For a while, it felt like we had a real chance. Then things broke down. There was debt, a split in the team, and another ending I hadn't planned for.

I kept building. With another team, I made websites and apps for clients. We brought in money. We grew. I also saw salaries go unpaid and people leave. I was exhausted and broke. I argued with people I cared about. Some of those relationships did not come back.

Later, I put time and money into a new product with a team that believed in it. That failed too.

I knew the list of things I'd done wrong. I hadn't planned well enough. I'd trusted without checking. I'd made promises before I knew how to keep them. None of that made it easier to look people in the eye when things went bad.

I asked myself if I was competent enough to lead anyone. If I was skilled enough to build something that could last. There were days when I didn't want another lesson. I wanted the work, the money, and the people back.

## The part of the Mahabharata I return to

Before the battle, Arjuna looks across the field and sees people he knows. He lowers his bow. Krishna speaks to him there, while he is unsure he can go on.

I think about that scene when I am tired of starting over. I don't compare my losses to Arjuna's battle. I remember that he was afraid and full of doubt, and that Krishna did not ask him to pretend otherwise.

> “You have the right to work, but never to its fruits.”
>
> Bhagavad Gita 2.47, a common English rendering of Krishna's words to Arjuna

I used to hear that as advice to stop caring whether I succeeded. I can't do that. The people who worked with me needed to be paid. The decisions I made had real costs. I still care very much about the result.

What helps me is remembering where I have a choice. I can own the mistakes I made. I can be more careful with money and with other people's trust. I can do the work in front of me, even when I cannot know how it will end.

![A restrained illustration of Arjuna's lowered bow and Krishna beside him at dawn, seen from behind](/blog-illustrations/i-lost-the-startup-kurukshetra.png)

## Starting again

I wish I could say I've made peace with all of it. I haven't. I still think about the people who left, the money we lost, and the things I should have said sooner.

For a time I thought losing so much meant I had no business building again. But the wish to make something useful never left. I can feel it even on the days I have to work alone.

I don't know if the next thing will work. I know I want to give it a more honest try: ask harder questions, make fewer promises, pay attention to the people doing the work with me, and face a problem before it becomes a crisis.

That's what failure has given me so far. I would rather have learned some of it another way. I can't. So I'm taking what I know now and building again.
