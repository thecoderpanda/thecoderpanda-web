An owner buys an AI subscription. Someone in the office tries a few prompts. A week later, the team is back to copying details from WhatsApp into a spreadsheet. The subscription stays on the card.

It is easy to see how this happens. Nobody has decided which job the tool should help with, who will check its work, or when the team will learn to use it.

For an Indian SME or MSME, I would start with one recurring task. Find something that takes too long, try AI on a small part of it, and count the time spent checking the result. The owner should be able to see whether it helped before paying for more.

## Start with work people already understand

Consider a small electrical-parts distributor. This is a made-up example, not a customer case study. Its sales assistant receives enquiries on WhatsApp, looks up product details, asks about missing quantities, and prepares quotations for approval.

AI could turn a customer message into a draft list of items and quantities. It could also draft a short reply asking for missing details. The assistant would still check product codes, confirm prices in the approved price list, and get the quotation signed off.

If the customer asks for “the same cable as last time”, AI cannot safely guess what that means. The assistant needs to look up the previous order or ask the customer. A useful tool should make that gap easier to spot.

![A sales assistant turns a sample enquiry into a draft, checks it against a price list, and approves the reply before sending it.](/blog-illustrations/ai-sme-daily-work.svg)

Choose a task where someone can check the answer without spending longer than the original job took. Drafting a follow-up message is a reasonable starting point. Letting AI decide credit limits or submit GST returns is a much bigger risk.

Some tasks need a simpler fix. If everyone types the same reply, a saved template may be enough. If totals are wrong, fix the spreadsheet formula. You do not have to use AI to make the trial worthwhile.

## If the business has never used AI

A company that runs on paper, Tally and phone calls does not need to replace all three before it can try anything. It does need a reliable source for the information used in the trial.

For quotations, that might mean agreeing on one current price list. For dispatch updates, it might mean keeping an accurate list of orders and delivery dates. If nobody knows which file is correct, AI will make it easier to circulate the wrong answer.

Ask the team whether anyone already uses an AI tool for work. Make clear that you are asking so you can agree on safe use, rather than catch someone out. Otherwise, people may keep experimenting on personal accounts with documents the business has never approved for upload.

![Four possible starting points: no AI use, individual trials, a shared team process, and wider use after review. These are stages, not survey results.](/blog-illustrations/ai-sme-maturity-chart.svg)

There is no need to rush through these stages. A small firm may get enough value from one well-run task for several months.

## Six steps to adopt AI in a team

### 1. Pick a task and record how it works today

Sit with the person doing the work. Watch a few ordinary cases, including an awkward one. Ask where they lose time and what they have to double-check.

Write down the time taken, the number of corrections, and who approves the result. You need this starting point to judge the trial later. Measuring only the AI version tells you very little.

For the distributor, the task could be preparing the first draft of a quotation. Keep price approval, credit decisions and sending the final quote outside the trial.

### 2. Choose two people and set a spending limit

Choose someone who knows the task and someone who is willing to learn the tool. They can be the same person in a very small business, but a second person should review the results.

Agree on a 30-day trial, a spending limit, and time to practise during working hours. Put one person in charge of collecting examples and problems. Give the owner a date when they will see the results.

Avoid connecting the tool to the full customer database at this stage. Start with invented or carefully anonymised examples. Removing a customer's name alone may not hide their identity if the message still contains their phone number, address or order details.

### 3. Agree on data rules before uploading anything

The team needs to know which tool is approved and what they can put into it. A paid subscription does not automatically make every upload safe. Check the provider's terms for data storage, model training and account access. If those terms are unclear, keep company records out.

![Three data-safety categories: public or invented material for practice, internal material only after approval, and sensitive records to keep out of a general-purpose AI trial.](/blog-illustrations/ai-sme-data-safety.svg)

For a first trial, use public product descriptions, dummy enquiries and sample messages. Keep Aadhaar copies, passwords, bank details, payroll records and customer lists out of general-purpose chat tools. Internal prices or supplier contracts need a separate approval decision and suitable data controls.

Name the person who checks every customer-facing answer. AI can invent a delivery date or discount even when the writing sounds confident. If someone uploads sensitive information by mistake, they should report it immediately so the business can assess what was exposed and take action.

### 4. Teach the task using a real working session

Give the team a small example they can complete together. For the distributor, an instruction might be:

> Turn this sample enquiry into a list of products and quantities. List anything missing separately. Do not guess product codes, prices, stock or delivery dates. Draft a short reply asking for the missing details.

Then check the answer against the original enquiry. Deliberately include an unclear product name so people see how the tool handles uncertainty. Save an example of a good result and an example that needed correction.

Let people practise in the language they use at work, whether that is English, Hindi or another Indian language. Check translated product names and technical terms carefully. A fluent reply can still name the wrong part.

A weekly 20-minute session is enough to compare attempts and update the shared instructions. Nobody should have to hide a failed attempt to look competent.

### 5. Count the checking time too

Suppose a quotation takes 20 minutes manually. During the trial, preparing the input takes four minutes, AI drafting takes two, and checking and correcting takes six. The new total is 12 minutes, so the saving is eight minutes per quotation.

These are example numbers, not a promised result. At 100 similar quotations a month, that would save about 13 hours and 20 minutes before training and setup time. Your result may be smaller, or the tool may take longer than the old process.

![An illustrative time comparison: 20 minutes manually versus 12 minutes with AI, including four minutes of preparation, two of drafting and six of review.](/blog-illustrations/ai-sme-time-comparison.svg)

Track errors as well as speed. Record missed items, wrong quantities and changes the reviewer had to make. Agree on an acceptable result before starting; do not lower the standard because a demo looked impressive.

Time saved is not automatically money saved. Salaries may stay the same. The benefit might be faster replies, less overtime or more time for overdue customer follow-ups. Compare that benefit with the subscription, training and review costs.

### 6. Decide whether to keep it

At the end of the month, show the owner a few before-and-after examples, the time records, the mistakes and the total cost. If checking takes too long or serious errors keep appearing, stop or narrow the task.

If it helps, save the instructions, name a backup reviewer and make the process easy for another employee to follow. Try a second task only when the first one works without the keenest person standing beside everyone.

## How to convince a “lala company” owner

People use “lala company” to describe an owner-led business where decisions depend heavily on the proprietor or family. The phrase can be dismissive. Here, I mean that way of running a business, not a judgement about a community or every family-owned firm.

These companies may have loyal customers, experienced staff and years of hard-earned knowledge. Their owners have also paid for software that nobody used. If you arrive saying they are behind, you give them another reason to distrust the proposal.

Start with a problem they already want solved. Perhaps quotations pile up when one employee is away. Perhaps the accounts team spends Friday evening drafting payment reminders. Ask permission to test a small change to that work.

A proposal could sound like this:

> “Can we try this on quotation drafts for 30 days? We will start with dummy enquiries and keep customer records out. The sales assistant will check every draft, and you will still approve prices. We will agree on a spending limit first. At the end, we will show you the time taken and the errors. If it adds work, we will stop.”

Show the owner a complete example, including the correction. A thirty-second demo that leaves out ten minutes of checking will only make the next conversation harder.

Bring the experienced munim, supervisor or sales assistant into the trial early. They know which customers have special terms and which product names people mix up. Ask them to help define the checks. Do not introduce the tool by suggesting their experience is now unnecessary.

Be honest about jobs. If the purpose of the trial is to reduce repetitive work, say so and explain where the saved time will go. Do not promise that roles will never change unless the business is prepared to stand behind that promise.

The owner's son or daughter might help, but choose the trial lead for their patience and understanding of the work. A long-serving employee who can explain mistakes calmly may be a better choice.

### Answer the objections without brushing them aside

| What the owner says | A useful response |
|---|---|
| “Our work is different.” | “Let's choose one repeatable part and test it against your own standards.” |
| “What happens to our data?” | “We will use dummy data first. No company records go in until we approve the tool and its terms.” |
| “My people won't use it.” | “Let's ask two volunteers to try it during working hours and tell us what gets in the way.” |
| “It makes mistakes.” | “Yes. We will record them and keep a reviewer. We will stop if the mistakes make the task unsafe or slower.” |
| “We are doing fine.” | “Then this can wait unless there is a specific problem worth the time and cost.” |

If someone from the same trade has run a useful trial, ask them to describe the work, cost and limits. Get their permission before sharing their results. A relevant example is helpful; an invented success story is not.

## What industry leaders' advice means for a small team

In a 2017 discussion reported by Stanford Engineering, Andrew Ng said:

> “AI needs to be customized for your business context.”

That is an excerpt from his answer about scarce AI talent. It predates today's chat tools and is not a claim about their results. For a small team, I take it as a reason to test the tool on the task you need done. A polished answer to a generic question tells you little about whether it can read your product enquiries accurately.

Sundar Pichai made room for caution in a 2018 Recode and MSNBC interview:

> “It's fair to be worried about AI. We want to be thoughtful about it.”

An owner asking about data or mistakes deserves a clear answer. So does an employee asking what happens to their job. You cannot settle those concerns by quoting a technology CEO. You need rules the business will follow and results the team can inspect.

The business example and time chart in this article are hypothetical; the stages and calendar are suggested ways to organise a trial, not survey findings.

## Where to start on Monday

Spend half an hour with the person who handles quotations, billing queries or customer follow-ups. Pick one task they would like help with and record how long a few cases take today.

Before buying a subscription, agree on the data rules, the reviewer and the spending limit. Put a review date on the calendar. At that meeting, show the work and decide whether to continue.
