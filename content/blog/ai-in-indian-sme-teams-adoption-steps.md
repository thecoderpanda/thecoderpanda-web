Most Indian businesses will not adopt AI through a grand transformation programme. They will adopt it because one accountant stopped retyping invoices, or a sales manager got her follow-ups done before lunch. AI succeeds in a company when it lands in a team's daily work, and a tool bought by the owner does not do that on its own.

![MSME numbers](/blog-illustrations/ai-msme-numbers.svg)

## Why this matters for MSMEs

India has roughly 63 million MSMEs, and they carry a large share of jobs, exports and GDP. They are also short on exactly what AI gives back: time. The owner is the sales head, the HR department and the complaint desk. A team of ten that gets two hours back per person per week gains the equivalent of a part-time hire, without the salary.

Industry leaders keep making the same point in different words.

> "AI is the new electricity."
>
> Andrew Ng, 2017

Electricity did not reach factories as a strategy. It arrived as a motor on one machine, and the businesses that wired up early compounded the advantage for decades.

> "AI is one of the most important things humanity is working on. It is more profound than, I dunno, electricity or fire."
>
> Sundar Pichai, 2018

You do not need to share that scale of excitement to see the practical point. Whatever your view on the hype, your competitors' clerks will soon be using it.

## Six steps to adopt AI in a team

![Six step roadmap](/blog-illustrations/ai-sme-roadmap.svg)

1. **Pick one pain, not one technology.** Ask each team: what repetitive task do you dread every week? Quotation drafting, GST reconciliation follow-ups, WhatsApp query replies and job-description writing are typical answers. Start from the pain.
2. **Run a 30-day pilot with one owner.** One team, one tool, one named person responsible. A pilot with no owner is a hobby.
3. **Measure something boring.** Hours saved per week, turnaround time, number of errors. If you cannot count it, you cannot defend the next rupee spent.
4. **Write simple rules.** What data never goes into a public tool (customer KYC, bank details, unreleased pricing)? Who reviews AI output before it reaches a customer? One page is enough.
5. **Let peers teach peers.** A clerk showing another clerk beats any external training session. Run a weekly 20-minute "what worked" huddle.
6. **Scale to the next workflow.** Only after the first one is stable. Resist buying five tools in a month.

## The lala company problem

Every city has them: the old-school, family-run business with zero AI. Ledgers, a trusted munim, a WhatsApp group that runs dispatch. These companies are often very profitable, and their owners are not fools. Their caution is rational: the business has survived three generations by not chasing fashions.

So the pitch cannot be "you are behind". It has to respect what they built.

**What actually works with them**

- **Start with the thing they already hate.** Reconciling statements, chasing payments, writing the same reply forty times. Never start with "AI strategy".
- **Show, do not tell.** Take one of their own documents, say a messy supplier invoice or a long customer email, and have AI summarise it in front of them in thirty seconds. Demos on their own data beat any slide deck.
- **Frame it as leverage for the munim, not a replacement.** The fear is job loss and loss of control. Promise, and mean it, that the first goal is removing drudgery, with a human checking every output.
- **Keep the downside tiny.** A free or low-cost tool, one team, one month. "If it does not save time, we stop" is an easy yes.
- **Use peer proof.** A competitor or a fellow trader association member who saved time carries more weight than any consultant. Bring a case from the same trade.
- **Speak in their units.** Not "productivity gains" but "two hours less per day on billing", or "one fewer person needed to hire this year".
- **Let the next generation lead, quietly.** In many family firms the son or daughter back from college is the natural pilot owner. Give them air cover from the senior owner and a small, visible win.

**Handling the common objections**

| Objection | Honest answer |
|---|---|
| "Our work is too specific." | Then start with the generic parts: emails, invoices, summaries. |
| "What about our data?" | Fair. Write the rules first and keep sensitive data out. |
| "My people won't use it." | Pick the one person who is curious. Ignore the rest for now. |
| "It makes mistakes." | So do tired humans. Keep a person reviewing the output. |
| "We are doing fine." | Yes, and the aim is to keep it that way as costs and competitors change. |

## What leaders get wrong

The common failure is buying a tool and expecting a culture to appear. Leaders announce an AI initiative, give no time to learn it, and judge it after a month by whether the numbers jumped. Adoption is a management problem, in the same way that adopting email or a new ERP was.

Give people permission to try, time to try, and a safe place to share what failed. Then the tool matters much less than the habit.

## Where to start on Monday

Pick one team. Ask them for their most hated weekly task. Try an AI tool on it for two weeks. Count the hours. Share the result at the next meeting, whatever it is.

That is the whole programme, and it is enough to put a company on the path.
